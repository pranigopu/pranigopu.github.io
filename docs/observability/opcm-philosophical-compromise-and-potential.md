# Philosophies underlying the technologies
The goals of "standarisation using OTel" and "centralisation of telemetry using Prometheus" is already very granular. It is like describing an animal in terms of anatomical structure; accurate, on one level, but incomplete, because reality has many levels of identity and causality, such that certain levels explain many specifics at once, and knowing these levels gives us power in reality. Consider how this like knowing that the specific symptoms of a disease are caused by germs, which in turn are identified as living beings, which means they can be dealt with as living beings (e.g. by killing/fighting them with disinfectant, antibodies, etc.).

What explains my approach to standarising and centralising telemetry? The clue lies in the philosophy behind OTel and Prometheus, because their intersection is what has produced this project. As discussed in ["Intent", OTel](./otel.md#intent), OTel was motivated by scale-induced opacity, i.e. the inability to correlate and causally link signals across a distributed system often with nested subsystems. Like a data ingestion pipeline from source to proxy servers to distributed streaming to distributed storage. Or an agentic workflow that involves a plan, tool calls, rerouting to other agents, etc. Or, like a web search fanning into requests to many query servers, spell-checking, subsystems to manage image and video searches, etc. Dapper from Google was the origin that eventually led (with many steps in-between) to OTel as a framework to enable the propagation and tracing of application-level, execution-scoped, telemetry-based context across a distributed system. The way I understand it, OTel's epistemological stance is this: you cannot know beforehand what information you need for insight, and thus, you must define the means by which information and its context can be reliably captured, shared, and interpreted, at arbitrary scale and granularity.

Prometheus, on the other hand, is a toolkit that is based on the idea that certain known unknowns must be proactively captured, collected, and acted upon. Its intent and design philosophy has been discussed in ["Intent", Prometheus](./prometheus.md#intent), and drawing from this, we see that its epistemological stance (if ideally practiced) is: we know what we want to know, we know why it is relevant, and we know what to do with it. And, again drawing from the aforementioned discussion, there is an important commitment Prometheus makes in its very design, given its design is focused on proactively pulling, organising, and ordering time-series metrics at ingestion time: focused reliability. It is focused around guaranteeing that the exact samples you seek will be available in the expected intervals and in the expected order. Considering this along with its lightweight design, we see that Prometheus is built to stand even if everything else falls, ensuring that the selected information we need about what is going on in the system are there especially when failures strike the system.

# Compromising both, fulfilling neither
Now, the architecture and approach of my project shifts the proactive pulling away from Prometheus and into a remote system - an OTel Collector with a receiver to gather metrics, and an exporter to send these to Prometheus. It turns Prometheus from a torch shining light into the system to an altar receiving light from the system. From proactive to passive. It is almost a reversal of what it was meant to do. What is the sense behind this sacrilege?

The core motivation was essentially just this: empower our system with the ability to use OTel for metrics. The initial idea - to use ClickHouse as the sink, with logs also being stored here as well through its own OTel pipeline - was aligned with OTel's philosophy, at least in spirit. In honesty, the move from ClickHouse to Prometheus as the aggregator was, in some sense, a compromise. Prometheus is cheap, efficient, battle-tested as an aggregator (even if it was meant to be more), and our dashboards and alerts already use it. The flattening of OTel attributes (e.g. resource attributes) to labels and the push to keep our footprint low (thereby minimise high-churn labels) further pushed us away from OTel's principles, bringing us to a strange middle-ground where we were trying to abide by Prometheus' principles while motivated by the desire to empower our systems with OTel. What we lose, thus, is the epistemological rigour of accounting for unknown unknowns, empowering novel insights and arbitrarily-scalable transparency. But what do we gain? This is what we shall now discuss.

# The unexpected gain: push-based vs. pull-based
Through this compromise, another motivation arose: push-based aggregation, vs. the pull-based approach we had. This motivation arose out of the specific shape of our infrastructure. It is a highly distributed infrastructure, with 4 EKS clusters spanning 2 regions, each with its own applications, workflows, pipelines, and peered/connected external sources (e.g. Confluent Cloud). To use Prometheus to aggregate metrics across this system, we could empower one Prometheus instance (perhaps pooled into a cluster of instances) to pull data remotely across region-and-environment boundaries (thereby placing a massive load in one cluster, or requiring a separate infrastructure, not to mention managing multiple security boundaries and networking complexity). Alternatively, we could do what we had done, and have localised aggregations via Prometheus scraping within their cluster, which are in turn aggregated by a central Prometheus scraping its own cluster as well as the "federate" endpoints exposed by the localised Prometheus instances. As for security boundaries, these were managed by making each Prometheus’ endpoint public, a questionable decision, security-wise.

To sum up the practical outlook, for our infrastructure, a pull-based approach in this manner involves layers of exposure (the centre must be able to access each remote source reliably), redundancy (e.g. having 4 separate aggregators for enabling one central aggregation), and computational load (every level of aggregation is based on scrapes).

Now, there are a number of things we can say about cost comparisons and architectural benefits between push-based and pull-based approaches, and while these are valid, these, to me, are one layer too granular, too derivative. The layer that explains the decision is this: elegance. More precisely, it is the divergence between the need stated simply, and the design itself. Conceptually, the need is: receive metrics from remote sources into one aggregator. Practically, the design is to aggregate metrics locally, then aggregate from these aggregators in a central aggregator. The push-based architecture’s design, in response to this, is almost a word-for-component translation of the need. Elegance was not the goal, initially, but it emerged, almost unexpectedly. It does not explain the initial push toward OTel, of course, but it explains the operational viability we saw before deciding to push further with this compromise. A secondary driver of this project as a whole, subservient to OTel-based options, but primary within the “push-based” narrative taken on its own terms.

To sum up the practical outlook, for our infrastructure, a push-based approach in this manner involves 1 exposed receiver (which allows writes from the remote locations), 1 aggregator (1 at the center), and computational load being divided into collection (at the remote locations) and aggregation (at the central location). Collection does not aggregate, and aggregation does not collect. This means for the aggregator, there is no service discovery, no scrape loops (which are especially costly for a high number of targets and high-cardinality scrapes), but still a lot of work to do, e.g. decompressing compressed remote write streams, WAL for persistence, handling out-of-order samples. Collection loads are localised and thus distributed, matching the distributed nature of the infrastructure.

# Philosophy and practicality
So far, we have looked at two angles: (1) the philosophical compromise, and (2) the practical gain. However, I think my project is not summed up by these two points, because in subverting the intent of both OTel and Prometheus, we achieve a synthesis with unique stance: reliably handling known unknowns (as Prometheus intends) as a stepping stone toward being able to handle unknown unknowns (as OTel intends). OTel's scope can be narrowed down to suit Prometheus' intent, but the infrastructure to leverage OTel's potential is already in place, and the commitment to using Prometheus is not a lasting commitment, because by design (as per the architecture, and judging by the previous version of the project using ClickHouse as the sink), downstream components such as the metrics aggregation are swappable components. In this view, my project is less of a compromise and more of a setup whose scope is narrowed for stability, but extensible by design.

# Conclusion
So, is my push-based, modular approach better than Prometheus' native pull-based architecture? It is at least no worse inherently, it may be better in practice, and it has the benefit of being more intuitive, easier to think about (due to separation of concerns) and easier to maintain/update (once set up). And most importantly, it leaves to door open for the use of OTel, because, ultimately, Prometheus is swappable and extensible as an aggregator. We are pursuing the ideal of OTel's philosophy, practically aligning with Prometheus’s philosophy, but in the process, getting an emergent value out of the elegance of push-based architecture.
